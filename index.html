The American alligator

An ancient wetland hunter — armored, patient, and essential to the southern marsh.

Explore the species View the gallery

Species

Built for the blackwater

Alligator mississippiensis has held these waters for millions of years — ambush predator, nest architect, and keystone of the swamp.

The American alligator is one of only two living alligator species on Earth (the other is the Chinese alligator). Adults are heavy, broad-snouted reptiles with a dark olive-to-black hide that disappears into tannin-stained water. Males commonly reach 11–13 feet; exceptional animals push past 14. Females are smaller, often 8–10 feet, and invest enormous care in nest and young.

Unlike many reptiles that simply endure a landscape, alligators reshape it. Their winter dens and summer “gator holes” hold water through drought, becoming lifelines for fish, turtles, wading birds, and mammals. Where alligators thrive, wetlands tend to stay richer and more resilient.

Life cycle

From mound to marsh

Spring bellows, summer nests, and autumn escorts — alligator parenting is one of the most attentive in the reptile world.

Courtship peaks in spring. Males bellow with a low, vibrating roar that ripples the water around their bodies — a display of size and territory. After mating, the female builds a nest mound of vegetation and mud, often 2–3 feet high, and lays 20–50 eggs inside the warm compost.

Nest temperature decides sex: warmer cores tend to produce males; cooler edges, females. After about 65 days, hatchlings chirp from inside the eggs. The mother opens the mound, sometimes carries young to water in her jaws, and may guard them for a year or more — rare devotion among crocodilians.

Hunt

Patient as the tide

Stillness is the strategy. Then a surge of muscle ends the wait in a single strike.

Alligators are opportunistic carnivores. Hatchlings start on insects, spiders, and small fish. Juveniles graduate to frogs, snakes, and crustaceans. Large adults take fish, turtles, birds, raccoons, deer, and occasionally livestock that wander too close to the bank. They do not chew — they crush, shake, and swallow, or spin in a “death roll” to tear larger prey.

Most hunting happens at dusk and night. Eyes and nostrils sit high on the skull so the body can vanish under a mirror-calm surface. Integumentary sense organs — tiny black dots along the jaws — detect pressure changes from a swimming fish or a stepping deer. Digestion is slow and efficient; a big meal can sustain an adult for days or weeks in cool weather.

Armor

A living fortress

Osteoderms under thick hide, a stealth float silhouette, and a bite force among the strongest measured in living animals.

  • Osteoderm armor

    Bony plates beneath the skin shield the back and help move heat after a long cool night on the bank.

  • Ambush senses

    Pressure receptors along the jaws detect the faintest ripple — dinner announces itself in the dark.

  • Powered by patience

    Long stillness conserves energy until one explosive strike decides the hunt.

Field notes

Alligator or crocodile?

In the U.S., only southern Florida hosts both — and the differences are readable at a glance once you know where to look.

American alligator

  • Broad, U-shaped snout
  • Darker olive-black coloration
  • Upper teeth hide when the mouth closes
  • Prefers freshwater wetlands
  • Range: Carolinas through the Gulf Coast

American crocodile

  • Narrower, V-shaped snout
  • Lighter gray-tan coloration
  • Fourth lower tooth stays visible
  • Tolerates brackish and coastal water
  • U.S. range: mostly South Florida

Range

From the Carolinas to the Gulf

Freshwater marshes, cypress swamps, slow rivers, oxbow lakes, and even golf-course ponds — anywhere warm water meets soft banks.

Core strongholds include Florida, Louisiana, Georgia, and South Carolina, with populations through Alabama, Mississippi, Texas, Arkansas, and North Carolina. Alligators bask to raise body temperature and dig dens to survive cold snaps. Once hunted to the brink in parts of their range, they rebounded after federal protection in 1967 and remain a living barometer of wetland health.

Protect

Keep the marsh wild

Give alligators space, keep wetlands wet, and never feed a wild gator — a fed alligator is a dead alligator.

Most conflicts start with people closing distance for photos, leaving fish scraps, or walking pets along the water’s edge at dusk. Observe from a distance, keep trash secured, and support wetland conservation where you live. The American alligator’s recovery is a conservation success — staying out of its way helps keep it that way.
